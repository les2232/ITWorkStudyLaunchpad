# Common IT Words

## Goal

Learn common IT words you will hear as an IT work-study student.

This module is meant for students who may be new to IT. You do not need to memorize every term immediately. The goal is to build enough familiarity so conversations, tickets, training modules, and shadowing make more sense.

---

## Plain-Language Explanation

IT uses a lot of words that may sound confusing at first.

Words like **domain**, **SCCM**, **imaging**, **ticket**, **asset tag**, and **escalation** are common in IT departments, but most people do not learn them unless they have worked in IT before.

This module gives you a beginner-friendly glossary so you can understand the basics before moving into hands-on tasks.

---

## Why This Matters

Knowing the vocabulary helps you:

- Understand what IT techs are talking about
- Follow instructions more confidently
- Ask better questions
- Write better ticket notes
- Know when to stop and ask for help
- Feel less lost during your first few shifts

It is okay if these words are new. Learning the language of IT is part of the job.

---

## Key Terms

### IT

IT stands for **Information Technology**.

In a college or workplace, IT helps support technology such as computers, accounts, software, networks, printers, classrooms, and other systems.

---

### User

A **user** is the person using the technology or asking for help.

Examples:

- Student
- Faculty member
- Staff member
- Work-study student
- Guest or visitor

When writing ticket notes, it is helpful to explain who was affected, if appropriate.

---

### Device

A **device** is a piece of technology.

Examples:

- Desktop computer
- Laptop
- Monitor
- Printer
- Keyboard
- Mouse
- Docking station
- Projector
- Phone
- Tablet

---

### Workstation

A **workstation** usually means the computer setup someone uses to work.

A workstation may include:

- Computer
- Monitor
- Keyboard
- Mouse
- Docking station
- Cables
- Desk location

Example:

> The front desk workstation monitor is not displaying.

---

### Account

An **account** is what someone uses to log into systems.

Examples:

- Computer login
- Email account
- Teams account
- Ticketing system account
- Timekeeping account

Do not try to fix account access issues on your own unless you have been specifically trained and authorized.

---

### Domain

A **domain** is a managed environment used by an organization to control accounts, computers, and access.

In simple terms:

> A domain helps the organization manage who can log in, which computers belong to the organization, and what resources people can access.

As a new work-study student, you do not need to manage the domain. You just need to understand that it is part of how school/work computers and accounts are controlled.

---

### Network

A **network** connects computers and devices so they can communicate.

Examples of things that may use the network:

- Internet access
- Shared drives
- Printers
- Login systems
- Software deployment
- Classroom technology

If something is not working, IT techs may ask whether the device is connected to the network.

---

### Permissions

**Permissions** control what someone is allowed to access or do.

Examples:

- Can this user open a shared folder?
- Can this student log into the ticketing system?
- Can this employee install software?
- Can this account access a specific application?

Permission issues should usually be escalated to a mentor, IT tech, or supervisor.

---

### MFA

**MFA** stands for **Multi-Factor Authentication**.

It means a user needs more than just a password to log in. They may also need to approve a sign-in on a phone, app, text message, or other method.

As a new work-study student, do not try to bypass MFA or change someone’s MFA settings unless you are specifically trained and authorized.

---

### Ticket

A **ticket** is a record of an issue, request, task, or support interaction.

A ticket helps IT track:

- Who needs help
- What the issue is
- What has already been tried
- Who is working on it
- Whether the issue is resolved
- What still needs to happen

A ticket is not just paperwork. It helps the whole team stay organized.

---

### Ticket Notes

**Ticket notes** explain what happened while working on a ticket.

Good ticket notes usually include:

- What the issue was
- What you checked
- What happened
- What you changed, if anything
- Whether the issue was resolved
- What still needs to be done
- Who it was escalated to, if applicable

Weak ticket note:

> Fixed.

Better ticket note:

> Checked keyboard issue at front desk workstation. Verified USB connection. Tested with known-working keyboard and confirmed replacement worked. Moved original keyboard to workbench for review.

---

### Asset Tag

An **asset tag** is a label or ID used to track equipment.

Many organizations use asset tags to track:

- Computers
- Monitors
- Printers
- Laptops
- Projectors
- Other technology

Do not remove or change asset tags unless instructed by a mentor or supervisor.

---

### Inventory

**Inventory** is the system or process used to track equipment.

Inventory may include:

- Device name
- Asset tag
- Serial number
- Location
- Assigned user
- Device status

Example:

> Before moving a computer, check whether the inventory record needs to be updated.

---

### Peripheral

A **peripheral** is an external device connected to a computer.

Examples:

- Keyboard
- Mouse
- Monitor
- Webcam
- Headset
- Printer
- Docking station

Swapping a basic peripheral may be beginner-safe if your department allows it.

---

### Docking Station

A **docking station** or **dock** lets a laptop connect to multiple devices at once.

A dock may connect to:

- Monitor
- Keyboard
- Mouse
- Network cable
- Power
- USB devices

If a monitor, keyboard, or mouse is not working, the dock may be part of the issue.

---

### Monitor Input or Source

A monitor may have multiple input/source options.

Examples:

- HDMI
- DisplayPort
- USB-C
- VGA

If a monitor says **No Signal**, one beginner-safe check is confirming the monitor is on the correct input/source.

---

### Imaging

**Imaging** is the process of preparing a computer with the required operating system, software, settings, and configuration.

In simple terms:

> Imaging gets a computer ready for use.

As a new work-study student, you may help prepare devices, move equipment, observe the process, or document status. You should not guess which image or workflow to use.

---

### Image

An **image** is a prepared computer setup that may include the operating system, software, settings, and configuration.

Different types of computers or departments may need different images.

Do not choose an image by guessing. Ask your mentor or the IT tech on duty.

---

### Task Sequence

A **task sequence** is a set of steps used during imaging or deployment.

It may tell the system what to install, configure, or run.

As a new work-study student, you do not need to manage task sequences. You should understand that they are part of the imaging process and should not be changed without permission.

---

### SCCM / Configuration Manager

**SCCM** is an older name many people still use for **Configuration Manager**.

IT departments may use Configuration Manager to manage computers, deploy software, run updates, and help with imaging.

For your role, you mostly need to understand what it is used for and when to ask a mentor before touching imaging-related steps.

---

### Deployment

A **deployment** is when software, updates, settings, or an operating system are sent out to computers.

Example:

> IT deployed software to a group of lab computers.

---

### Troubleshooting

**Troubleshooting** means figuring out what is wrong and what to try next.

Beginner troubleshooting usually starts with safe checks, such as:

- Is it plugged in?
- Is it powered on?
- Are cables connected?
- Is the monitor on the right input?
- Does a known-working keyboard or mouse work?
- What error message appears?

Troubleshooting does not mean guessing. It means checking carefully and documenting what happened.

---

### Escalation

**Escalation** means asking someone with more experience, access, or authority to help.

You should escalate when:

- You are unsure what workflow applies
- The issue involves accounts or permissions
- The task involves sensitive data
- Imaging fails
- You see an unfamiliar error
- You are not sure which device, image, or inventory record to use
- Beginner-safe checks did not fix the issue
- You feel stuck

Escalating is not failure. It is part of safe IT work.

---

### Mentor / Buddy

A **mentor** or **buddy** is the person assigned to help you learn.

Ask your mentor when:

- You are confused
- You are unsure what to do next
- You do not know whether a task is safe
- You need help understanding a procedure
- You want feedback on ticket notes

---

### Workbench

A **workbench** is an area where IT devices may be inspected, prepared, repaired, imaged, or reviewed.

Do not move equipment to or from the workbench unless you have been instructed to do so.

---

### Documentation

**Documentation** is written information that explains what happened, what steps to follow, or what decisions were made.

Examples:

- Ticket notes
- How-to guides
- Checklists
- Inventory notes
- Training modules
- Escalation summaries

Good documentation helps the whole team.

---

## Common Beginner Mistakes

New IT work-study students may accidentally:

- Pretend they understand instead of asking
- Guess which workflow applies
- Use words they do not fully understand yet
- Write ticket notes that are too vague
- Skip documentation
- Continue after an unfamiliar error
- Forget to ask whether a task is beginner-safe
- Assume every computer uses the same imaging process

These mistakes are preventable. The safest habit is:

> Stop, document what you know, and ask your mentor.

---

## When to Ask for Help

Ask your mentor, buddy, IT tech, or supervisor when:

- You do not understand a term
- You are unsure what procedure applies
- You see an unfamiliar error
- You are working with accounts, permissions, imaging, or sensitive data
- You are unsure whether you are allowed to do something
- A user is waiting and you do not know what to say
- You feel embarrassed but still need clarification

It is better to ask early than to guess.

## Quick Check

Answer these in your own words.

1. What is a ticket?
2. What is an asset tag used for?
3. What does imaging mean?
4. What should you do if you do not know which image to use?
5. What is escalation?
6. Why are clear ticket notes important?

---

## Strong Answers

1. A ticket is a record of an issue, request, task, or support interaction.
2. An asset tag is used to identify and track equipment.
3. Imaging prepares a computer with the required operating system, software, settings, and configuration.
4. Do not guess. Ask your mentor or the IT tech on duty.
5. Escalation means asking someone with more experience, access, or authority to help.
6. Clear ticket notes help the IT team understand what happened, what was checked, and what still needs to be done.
