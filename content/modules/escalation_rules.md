# Escalation Rules

## Goal

Understand when to stop, document, and ask for help instead of continuing alone.

Escalation is one of the most important safety habits for a new IT work-study student. It protects users, devices, data, access, and your own learning.

---

## Plain-Language Explanation

Escalation means asking someone with more experience, access, or authority to help.

In simple terms:

> Escalation is how you bring the right person into the problem.

Escalating does not mean you failed. It means you recognized that the task needs support, approval, or a higher access level.

---

## Why This Matters

Some IT tasks are safe for beginners, while others can affect important systems.

Escalation matters because the wrong action can:

- Lock a user out.
- Change permissions incorrectly.
- Affect user data.
- Configure the wrong computer.
- Break an imaging workflow.
- Create security risk.
- Make the issue harder to fix.

The safest pattern is:

> Stop, document what happened, and ask for help.

---

## Key Terms

### Escalation

Escalation means asking someone with more experience, access, or authority to help.

---

### Beginner-Safe

Beginner-safe tasks are tasks a new work-study student may do after instruction because they carry lower risk.

Examples may include checking a cable, writing down an error message, or preparing a draft summary.

---

### Approval

Approval means a mentor, IT tech, or supervisor has confirmed that you may perform a task.

Approval may depend on training, timing, user impact, or department procedure.

---

### Sensitive Data

Sensitive data is information that should be protected.

Examples may include passwords, student records, employee information, personal files, or private business information.

Do not access, copy, delete, or share sensitive data unless trained and authorized.

---

### Permissions

Permissions control what someone is allowed to access or do.

Permission changes should be handled by trained and authorized staff.

---

### Unknown Error

An unknown error is an error message, code, or behavior you do not recognize.

Write it down exactly and ask for help.

---

### Mentor Summary

A mentor summary is a short explanation of what happened, what you checked, and what help you need.

It can help your mentor decide the next step quickly.

---

## What You Need to Know

As a new IT work-study student, you should understand these basic ideas:

1. Escalation is expected.
2. Some tasks require approval even if they seem simple.
3. Account, permission, MFA, imaging, and data issues usually require escalation.
4. Unknown errors should be documented exactly.
5. Do not keep trying random fixes.
6. A good escalation includes the issue, checks, result, and question.
7. If you are unsure whether to escalate, escalate.

---

## Beginner-Safe Things You Can Do

Depending on department procedure, beginner-safe actions may include:

- Pause the task when something unexpected happens.
- Write down the exact error message.
- Record device name, asset tag, location, or user impact if appropriate.
- List what you were instructed to check.
- Explain what happened after each check.
- Prepare a short mentor summary.
- Ask what the next approved step is.

These actions make escalation useful without requiring risky changes.

---

## What You Should Not Do Without Approval

Do not do these unless trained and authorized:

- Reset passwords.
- Change account permissions.
- Bypass MFA.
- Delete user files.
- Use admin credentials.
- Choose an imaging task sequence by guessing.
- Rerun imaging repeatedly after an error.
- Change ticket/work-order status.
- Close a ticket.
- Tell a user the issue is resolved when you are unsure.

When in doubt, stop and ask.

---

## Common Beginner Mistakes

New work-study students may accidentally:

- Wait too long to escalate.
- Keep clicking after an unfamiliar prompt.
- Restart a process repeatedly.
- Leave out the exact error message.
- Guess because they want to be helpful.
- Assume a task is safe because it looks simple.
- Forget to mention what they already checked.
- Feel like escalation means they did something wrong.

Escalation is a normal part of IT teamwork.

---

## When to Ask for Help

Ask your mentor, IT tech, or supervisor when:

- You are unsure what workflow applies.
- A user cannot log in.
- MFA, passwords, permissions, or accounts are involved.
- Imaging fails or shows an unfamiliar message.
- Data may be deleted, moved, copied, or exposed.
- Equipment does not match the ticket or asset record.
- A user is upset or needs an answer you cannot confirm.
- You are unsure whether the issue is resolved.
- You are tempted to guess.

---

## Example Student Questions

### "When should I escalate?"

Escalate when you are unsure, when the task requires access or approval, when sensitive data may be involved, or when an unfamiliar error appears.

---

### "What should I say when escalating?"

Use this pattern:

> I am working on ____. The issue is ____. I checked ____. The result was ____. I stopped because ____. What should I do next?

---

### "What if it seems like an easy fix?"

If the fix involves access, settings, data, accounts, imaging, or ticket status, ask first. Some risky tasks look simple from the outside.

---

## Quick Check

Answer these in your own words.

1. What does escalation mean?
2. Why is escalation important?
3. Name three situations that should usually be escalated.
4. What should you document before asking for help?
5. Why should you avoid repeated random fixes?
6. What should you do if you are unsure whether to escalate?

---

## Strong Answers

1. Escalation means asking someone with more experience, access, or authority to help.
2. It prevents mistakes and protects users, devices, data, access, and workflows.
3. Examples include account issues, permission problems, MFA issues, imaging errors, sensitive data, unknown errors, and tasks requiring approval.
4. Document the issue, exact error, device or asset tag, what you checked, what happened, and what help you need.
5. Random fixes can make the issue worse or hide useful information.
6. Escalate. It is better to ask early than to guess.
